# Common AI Writing Pitfalls

This section is the quick reference for patterns that often make writing feel generated, generic, or over-processed. These are not proof that text was written by AI. They are review signals.

The editing goal is simple:

> Keep the useful thought. Remove the synthetic polish.

## PIT-001 — Em dash overuse

Avoid:
> The tool saves time—it also improves visibility—it helps teams collaborate better.

Prefer:
> The tool saves time by showing who owns each request and where it is stuck.

Rule:
One em dash can work. Multiple em dashes in every paragraph start to feel like a writing habit instead of a choice.

Editor’s question:
Would a period, comma, colon, or sentence split make the relationship clearer?

---

## PIT-002 — Inflated verbs

Avoid:
> Leverage, empower, unlock, optimize, elevate, transform, revolutionize.

Prefer:
> Use, give, show, reduce, improve, replace, simplify.

Rule:
Use the smallest accurate verb unless the larger verb adds real meaning.

---

## PIT-003 — Abstract noun pileup

Avoid:
> This initiative supports alignment, visibility, efficiency, and transformation.

Prefer:
> This change lets Legal, Finance, and Operations review the same request record.

Rule:
If a sentence has three or more abstract nouns, look for the people, process, or decision underneath.

---

## PIT-004 — Constant positivity

Avoid:
> This exciting update gives teams a powerful new way to drive meaningful results.

Prefer:
> This update adds approval exports with comments, timestamps, and final decisions.

Rule:
AI often praises the thing before explaining it. Explain it first.

---

## PIT-005 — Fake importance

Avoid:
> It is important to understand that clear communication is essential for success.

Prefer:
> Communication breaks down when decisions are implied instead of written down.

Rule:
Do not announce importance. Show the consequence.

---

## PIT-006 — Throat-clearing openings

Avoid:
> In today’s rapidly evolving digital landscape, businesses must adapt to stay competitive.

Prefer:
> Email approvals stop working once more than one team needs the same record.

Rule:
Start with the actual problem, observation, or claim.

---

## PIT-007 — Generic balanced phrasing

Avoid:
> This approach not only improves efficiency but also enhances collaboration.

Prefer:
> The main benefit is fewer approval-status emails. The secondary benefit is cleaner audit history.

Rule:
“Not only... but also...” often creates symmetry where the idea needs ranking.

---

## PIT-008 — Robotic paragraph rhythm

Avoid:
> Every paragraph is four medium-length sentences with a topic sentence, explanation, example, and summary.

Prefer:
> Vary paragraph length based on the job of the paragraph. Some points need one sentence.

Rule:
Human writing changes pace when the thought changes.

---

## PIT-009 — Overexplaining the obvious

Avoid:
> Clear communication is important because it helps people understand information more effectively.

Prefer:
> The problem was not tone. The decision was never written down.

Rule:
If the sentence explains something every reader already knows, cut it or replace it with a specific case.

---

## PIT-010 — Generic transitions

Avoid:
> Furthermore, moreover, additionally, consequently, overall, in conclusion.

Prefer:
> Use transitions only when they clarify the relationship between ideas.

Rule:
Many paragraphs do not need a transition word. The next sentence can simply continue.

---

## PIT-011 — Fake nuance

Avoid:
> While this may seem simple, it is actually a complex and multifaceted issue that requires careful consideration.

Prefer:
> The hard part is not choosing the tool. It is agreeing on who approves exceptions.

Rule:
Nuance should identify the real complication.

---

## PIT-012 — “It depends” without a dependency

Avoid:
> It depends on your goals, needs, and unique situation.

Prefer:
> It depends on whether Legal needs to review every request or only exceptions above a dollar threshold.

Rule:
If the answer depends, name what it depends on.

---

## PIT-013 — List addiction

Avoid:
> This improves speed, clarity, collaboration, accountability, efficiency, visibility, scalability, and trust.

Prefer:
> This mainly improves two things: status visibility and audit history.

Rule:
Long lists often hide weak prioritization.

---

## PIT-014 — Overuse of three-part rhythm

Avoid:
> Clear, concise, and actionable.
> Fast, flexible, and scalable.
> Simple, secure, and powerful.

Prefer:
> Clear enough that the next step is obvious.

Rule:
Three-part lists can sound polished but empty when the words are interchangeable.

---

## PIT-015 — Generic summaries after every section

Avoid:
> Ultimately, the key takeaway is that communication is essential for success.

Prefer:
> Stop once the point is made, or end with the next practical action.

Rule:
Not every section needs a wrap-up sentence.

---

## PIT-016 — Synthetic empathy

Avoid:
> We understand how frustrating it can be to navigate the complexities of modern workflows.

Prefer:
> If approvals live in email, someone usually has to rebuild the status later.

Rule:
Empathy is stronger when it names the real inconvenience.

---

## PIT-017 — Vague expertise signals

Avoid:
> As an expert in the field, I can confidently say...

Prefer:
> In the last three implementations I reviewed, the same issue appeared: no one owned exception approvals.

Rule:
Credibility comes from observation, detail, and judgment.

---

## PIT-018 — Polished neutrality

Avoid:
> There are several factors to consider when evaluating this topic.

Prefer:
> The first question is whether the current process is slow because of approvals or because no one can see the status.

Rule:
Neutrality is useful only when it helps the reader think more clearly.

---

## PIT-019 — LinkedIn moralizing

Avoid:
> Leadership is not about titles. It is about impact.

Prefer:
> The best managers I have worked with write decisions down before the meeting ends.

Rule:
Replace universal lessons with specific behaviors.

---

## PIT-020 — YouTube retention bait

Avoid:
> Stay until the end because number five will surprise you.

Prefer:
> I tested five approaches. Two were useful. Three were not worth repeating.

Rule:
Earn retention with useful structure, not hostage language.

---

## PIT-021 — Blog intro definition trap

Avoid:
> Communication is the process of exchanging information between individuals or groups.

Prefer:
> Most workplace communication problems start after the meeting, when no one writes down what changed.

Rule:
Do not define familiar words unless the definition changes the argument.

---

## PIT-022 — Business jargon disguised as precision

Avoid:
> We need to operationalize cross-functional alignment.

Prefer:
> Product, Legal, and Finance need one owner, one deadline, and one place to approve the rollout.

Rule:
Technical language clarifies. Jargon makes familiar work sound abstract.

---

## PIT-023 — False humanization tricks

Avoid:
> Add random fragments, contractions, slang, and em dashes to make the draft sound human.

Prefer:
> Add concrete observations, uneven but readable rhythm, and real constraints.

Rule:
Human writing is not a costume. It is usually more specific, situated, and selective.

---

## PIT-024 — Overcorrecting into casualness

Avoid:
> Kinda just wanted to toss this out there because it’s super important.

Prefer:
> I want to flag one issue before we approve the timeline.

Rule:
Natural professional writing can be direct without becoming sloppy.

---

## PIT-025 — Safe conclusion language

Avoid:
> In conclusion, this highlights the importance of adopting a strategic approach.

Prefer:
> The decision is simple: either keep approvals in email and accept the audit gap, or move them into a tracked system.

Rule:
End with the consequence, decision, or next step.

---

## PIT-026 — Repeated sentence templates

Avoid:
> One of the biggest benefits of X is Y.
> One of the key challenges of X is Y.
> One of the most important aspects of X is Y.

Prefer:
> Name the point directly.

Rule:
Template repetition is often more noticeable than word repetition.

---

## PIT-027 — Over-polished certainty

Avoid:
> This will undoubtedly transform the way teams work.

Prefer:
> This should help teams that lose approval records in email.

Rule:
Use measured confidence. Do not promise more than the evidence supports.

---

## PIT-028 — The “delve” family

Avoid:
> Delve into, dive into, explore, unpack, shed light on, navigate, landscape, realm.

Prefer:
> Review, compare, test, explain, show, walk through.

Rule:
Many content-intro verbs sound like the writer is announcing an essay instead of saying something.

---

## PIT-029 — Punctuation as personality

Avoid:
> Using dashes, ellipses, parentheses, italics, and bolding as a substitute for voice.

Prefer:
> Use punctuation to clarify emphasis that is already earned by the sentence.

Rule:
Punctuation should support rhythm, not create fake spontaneity.

---

## PIT-030 — No visible tradeoff

Avoid:
> This gives teams control and speed without compromise.

Prefer:
> The product adds control by routing exceptions, but routine requests can still move automatically.

Rule:
Good business writing often admits the tradeoff and explains how it is handled.

---

# Prompt Library

Use these prompts to generate first drafts that are clearer, more factual, and less prone to generic AI patterns. The best prompts give the model a job, a voice standard, and a revision rule.

## PR-001 — Natural professional draft

Prompt:
> Draft this in a natural professional voice. Use plain language, specific nouns, and measured confidence. Avoid corporate buzzwords, motivational phrasing, exaggerated positivity, and generic transitions. Write like an experienced colleague explaining the point to one smart person.

Use when:
You need a clean first draft for business, email, LinkedIn, blog, or internal writing.

---

## PR-002 — Concrete rewrite

Prompt:
> Rewrite this so every vague claim becomes either a specific fact, a named action, a named person/team, or a measurable result. Keep the meaning. Remove inflated words unless they add precision.

Use when:
The draft says things like “improve efficiency,” “drive alignment,” or “unlock value.”

---

## PR-003 — Anti-LinkedIn pass

Prompt:
> Revise this so it does not sound like a generic LinkedIn leadership post. Remove fake vulnerability, engagement bait, moralizing, inflated lessons, and slogans. Replace them with specific observations, concrete examples, and practical implications.

Use when:
The draft sounds performative or motivational.

---

## PR-004 — YouTube conversational script

Prompt:
> Turn this into a YouTube script that sounds spoken, not narrated. Use short paragraphs, natural pauses, direct transitions, and specific examples. Avoid “in today’s video,” “let’s dive in,” “stay until the end,” and generic hype.

Use when:
You want a script that can be read aloud without sounding like a template.

---

## PR-005 — Marketing copy grounded in evidence

Prompt:
> Rewrite this marketing copy so it reveals value through tasks, outcomes, proof, and use cases. Do not manufacture excitement. Avoid “transform,” “revolutionize,” “game-changing,” “seamless,” “powerful,” and “innovative” unless the sentence proves the claim.

Use when:
You are writing landing pages, product pages, campaigns, or launch copy.

---

## PR-006 — Sales copy credibility pass

Prompt:
> Rewrite this sales message so it sounds credible and specific. Remove fake urgency, vague personalization, broad benefit claims, and pushy CTAs. Anchor the message in the buyer’s likely problem, one useful observation, and one clear next step.

Use when:
You are writing cold outreach, follow-up, objection handling, or sales DMs.

---

## PR-007 — Business writing precision pass

Prompt:
> Make this more precise without making it casual. Keep appropriate business and technical terms. Remove corporate fog. State the decision, owner, timing, risk, and next action wherever relevant.

Use when:
You are writing updates, recommendations, summaries, or internal documents.

---

## PR-008 — Remove synthetic polish

Prompt:
> Edit this for synthetic polish. Remove unnecessary summaries, balanced sentence templates, generic transitions, inflated positivity, and abstract nouns. Keep the strongest ideas and make the rhythm more natural.

Use when:
The draft is correct but feels too smooth or too generic.

---

## PR-009 — Fact-first rewrite

Prompt:
> Rewrite this so the fact comes before the interpretation. Lead with what happened, what changed, what was measured, or what someone observed. Put lessons or opinions after the evidence.

Use when:
The draft starts with principles, values, or conclusions instead of facts.

---

## PR-010 — Voice restraint prompt

Prompt:
> Keep the voice restrained. Do not oversell, over-explain, over-praise, or over-summarize. Use contractions only where they sound natural. Leave some transitions implicit.

Use when:
The draft is too enthusiastic or too polished.

---

## PR-011 — Technical but readable

Prompt:
> Keep technical terms that are necessary for accuracy. Explain only what a capable reader may not know. Remove unnecessary abstraction and replace vague category words with the actual system, process, metric, or decision.

Use when:
The audience is professional or technical and the writing still needs to feel human.

---

## PR-012 — Edit for spoken rhythm

Prompt:
> Rewrite this so it sounds natural when spoken aloud. Vary sentence length. Use occasional short sentences. Remove formal transitions that a person would not say. Keep the point clear and factual.

Use when:
The piece will become a video, podcast, webinar, or presentation script.

---

## PR-013 — Specificity audit

Prompt:
> Identify every vague phrase in this draft. For each one, suggest a more specific replacement using a person, team, action, metric, object, deadline, or example.

Use when:
You want a diagnostic pass before rewriting.

---

## PR-014 — Ending rewrite

Prompt:
> Rewrite the ending so it does not use a generic summary, motivational close, or “key takeaway” phrase. End with a concrete implication, decision, next step, or unresolved but useful question.

Use when:
The draft ends with ceremonial wrap-up language.

---

## PR-015 — Platform adaptation

Prompt:
> Adapt this for [platform]. Keep the core idea, but adjust the rhythm and structure for that platform. Do not add generic platform clichés. Preserve factual detail and remove any phrase that sounds like a template.

Use when:
You are moving the same idea between LinkedIn, YouTube, email, blog, sales, or internal business writing.

---

## PR-016 — Before/after generator

Prompt:
> Create five before/after rewrites from this draft. In each before example, quote the weak or generic sentence. In each after example, rewrite it in clear, natural, specific language. Add a one-sentence note explaining what changed.

Use when:
You are building training examples or improving a rough draft.

---

## PR-017 — Remove AI pitfall patterns

Prompt:
> Review this draft against the Common AI Writing Pitfalls section. Flag em dash overuse, inflated verbs, abstract noun pileups, constant positivity, fake nuance, generic transitions, synthetic empathy, and template repetition. Then rewrite only the sentences that need it.

Use when:
You want a targeted AI-pattern cleanup without flattening the whole voice.

---

## PR-018 — Preserve expertise

Prompt:
> Improve clarity without dumbing this down. Preserve technical accuracy, domain terms, and professional judgment. Only simplify phrases that are vague, inflated, redundant, or unnecessarily formal.

Use when:
The draft is technical, legal, financial, operational, or executive-facing.

---

# Pattern Emulation Library

This section defines positive patterns to emulate. The goal is not to imitate “human writing” in a gimmicky way. The goal is to use structures that naturally appear when people explain real work, real decisions, and real observations.

## PE-001 — Observation before lesson

Weak pattern:
> Communication is essential for successful teams.

Better pattern:
> The best teams I have worked with write decisions down before the meeting ends. That one habit prevents most handoff confusion.

Rule:
Start with what was seen. Then explain what it means.

---

## PE-002 — Specific situation before principle

Weak pattern:
> Good processes create accountability.

Better pattern:
> When every approval has one owner and one deadline, people stop asking who is responsible.

Rule:
Let the situation prove the principle.

---

## PE-003 — Narrow claim

Weak pattern:
> Automation improves efficiency.

Better pattern:
> Automation helps most when the same decision path repeats every week.

Rule:
Narrow claims are often more believable than universal claims.

---

## PE-004 — Measured confidence

Weak pattern:
> This will transform your workflow.

Better pattern:
> This should help if your team loses approval history in email.

Rule:
Use confidence that matches the evidence.

---

## PE-005 — Plain first sentence

Weak pattern:
> In today’s increasingly complex business environment, teams need a better way to manage work.

Better pattern:
> Email is a bad place to run approvals.

Rule:
A plain first sentence can carry more force than a polished setup.

---

## PE-006 — Useful contrast

Weak pattern:
> The solution improves both speed and control.

Better pattern:
> Routine requests move automatically. Exceptions still go to a reviewer.

Rule:
Contrast works when each side names a real behavior.

---

## PE-007 — Concrete concession

Weak pattern:
> While every organization is unique, this approach can help.

Better pattern:
> This will not fix unclear policies. It will help once the approval path is known.

Rule:
Concessions should clarify limits, not soften the sentence.

---

## PE-008 — One strong example

Weak pattern:
> This can be used across many industries and departments.

Better pattern:
> A finance team can use it to require a second approval on vendor requests above $25,000.

Rule:
One concrete example usually beats a broad list of possibilities.

---

## PE-009 — Process language

Weak pattern:
> We help teams collaborate more effectively.

Better pattern:
> Sales submits the request, Legal reviews the terms, and Finance approves payment without leaving the record.

Rule:
Show the sequence.

---

## PE-010 — Imperfect but honest rhythm

Weak pattern:
> This creates a seamless experience that enables teams to move faster and work smarter.

Better pattern:
> It is not fancy. It just keeps the approval, comments, and final decision in the same place.

Rule:
Sometimes a slightly plain sentence feels more trustworthy.

---

## PE-011 — Question that reveals the issue

Weak pattern:
> Teams need better visibility into workflows.

Better pattern:
> If someone asks who approved the request, can your team answer without searching email?

Rule:
Use questions when they expose the real test.

---

## PE-012 — Decision-first update

Weak pattern:
> After careful consideration, we have aligned on a path forward.

Better pattern:
> We are delaying the rollout until September so support can finish training.

Rule:
State the decision before the backstory.

---

## PE-013 — Spoken transition

Weak pattern:
> Furthermore, this raises another important consideration.

Better pattern:
> There is one catch.

Rule:
Use transitions people actually say.

---

## PE-014 — Specific uncertainty

Weak pattern:
> More research is needed.

Better pattern:
> We still do not know whether the delay is coming from manager review or legal review.

Rule:
Uncertainty is useful when it names the unknown.

---

## PE-015 — Ending with implication

Weak pattern:
> Ultimately, communication is key.

Better pattern:
> If the decision is not written down, the next person has to guess.

Rule:
End with what the reader should see differently.

---

## PE-016 — Direct comparison

Weak pattern:
> Our approach is more flexible than traditional systems.

Better pattern:
> A fixed workflow sends every request through the same path. This lets admins route exceptions differently.

Rule:
Comparison needs two visible sides.

---

## PE-017 — Human-scale measurement

Weak pattern:
> This significantly improves operational efficiency.

Better pattern:
> The team stopped spending Friday afternoon rebuilding the approval tracker.

Rule:
Not every metric needs to be a percentage. Human-scale detail can be enough.

---

## PE-018 — Constraint as credibility

Weak pattern:
> This works for any team.

Better pattern:
> This works best when the approval path is repeatable. It is less useful when every request needs a custom review.

Rule:
Naming fit makes the claim more trustworthy.

---

# Human Voice Examples

These examples show practical voice patterns that feel natural because they are specific, restrained, and situated. Use them as models, not scripts.

## HV-001 — Direct professional

Instead of:
> We are excited to announce a new enhancement that empowers users with greater visibility.

Use:
> We added export history. You can now download comments, timestamps, and final approval status from any request.

Why it works:
It says what changed before asking the reader to care.

---

## HV-002 — Conversational YouTube

Instead of:
> In today’s video, we’ll explore three powerful strategies for improving productivity.

Use:
> I tried three changes to my workflow last month. One was useless. One was fine. One actually stuck.

Why it works:
It sounds like a person reporting an experience, not a narrator opening a template.

---

## HV-003 — Plain LinkedIn observation

Instead of:
> Great leadership is about empowering others to become the best version of themselves.

Use:
> The best managers I have worked with make decisions easy to find later.

Why it works:
It replaces moral language with an observable behavior.

---

## HV-004 — Business update

Instead of:
> We have aligned on a strategic path forward to ensure a smooth transition.

Use:
> We are moving the launch to September. Support needs two more weeks to finish training.

Why it works:
It leads with the decision and explains the reason plainly.

---

## HV-005 — Marketing page

Instead of:
> Transform the way your team collaborates with a powerful all-in-one platform.

Use:
> Keep requests, approvals, comments, and final decisions in one record.

Why it works:
It shows the product through the work it handles.

---

## HV-006 — Sales follow-up

Instead of:
> Just checking in to see if this is still on your radar.

Use:
> Following up on the audit-history question from our demo. I can send the export example if that is still useful.

Why it works:
It connects the follow-up to the buyer’s actual concern.

---

## HV-007 — Blog opening

Instead of:
> In today’s fast-paced world, communication has never been more important.

Use:
> Most handoff problems start when the decision is clear in the meeting but never written down afterward.

Why it works:
It starts with a specific failure mode.

---

## HV-008 — Technical explanation

Instead of:
> This robust architecture enables scalable and secure data exchange.

Use:
> The API uses signed requests, retries failed jobs, and logs every status change.

Why it works:
It keeps the technical detail and removes the empty praise.

---

## HV-009 — Measured recommendation

Instead of:
> This is a game-changing opportunity that every organization should prioritize.

Use:
> This is worth testing if approval delays are creating rework every week.

Why it works:
It defines fit instead of overselling.

---

## HV-010 — Honest caveat

Instead of:
> This solution works seamlessly for teams of all sizes.

Use:
> This works best when the approval path is known. If every request is different, the setup will take longer.

Why it works:
It builds trust by naming the limit.

---

## HV-011 — Natural short ending

Instead of:
> Ultimately, the key takeaway is that organizations must embrace clarity to succeed.

Use:
> If the decision matters, write it down.

Why it works:
It ends with a usable rule instead of a ceremonial summary.

---

## HV-012 — Founder or operator voice

Instead of:
> We built this to revolutionize operational workflows for modern businesses.

Use:
> We built this because teams kept asking the same question: who approved this, and where is the record?

Why it works:
It ties the product to a real question customers ask.

---

## HV-013 — Calm objection handling

Instead of:
> I completely understand your concern, but our solution is designed to deliver unmatched value.

Use:
> That concern makes sense. If your current process already gives you clean audit history, this may not be urgent.

Why it works:
It does not fight the objection. It clarifies fit.

---

## HV-014 — Internal escalation

Instead of:
> We need to escalate this matter to ensure stakeholder alignment and timely resolution.

Use:
> We need Legal and Finance to decide today, or the launch date moves.

Why it works:
It names the decision, people, and consequence.

---

## HV-015 — Social post with restraint

Instead of:
> I am humbled and honored to share this incredible milestone with my amazing network.

Use:
> We shipped the first version today. It is small, but customers can finally export the approval record without asking support.

Why it works:
It keeps pride grounded in what changed for users.
